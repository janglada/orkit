    Modeling notes¶

    Practical advice and limits worth knowing before you rely on a result. Each section is short and stands on its own.

    Use whole numbers¶

    All quantities are whole numbers: durations, lengths, weights, costs, penalties. There are no decimals.

    If your data has decimals, pick a smaller unit so that it does not:

    Instead of Use Example
    1.5 hours minutes takes(90)
    2.35 meters millimeters length(2350)
    12.99 euros cents cost(1299)
    0.8 tonnes kilograms weight(800) 
    // Wrong idea: 2.5 kg does not fit in a whole number.
// Right: use grams.
.item("Laptop", i -> i.profit(1200).weight(2500))
.bin("Bag", b -> b.capacity(10_000))        // 10 kg

    Use the same unit everywhere in one model. Mixing minutes and hours, or kilograms and grams, gives plans that look valid but are wrong.

    Very large numbers. If your numbers are so large that a total would not fit in a Java long, orkit refuses the model with a ModelException whose message contains overflows. It never continues with a wrong total. Pick a coarser unit if this happens.

    How each domain measures time¶

    Domain How time works
    Job-shop Plain numbers by default: takes(10) means 10 units, and you decide what a unit is. Call startingAt(...) to work with real dates and times instead. See Calendar time.
    Scheduling Calendar dates. You give a start date and a number of days. Shifts have a start and end time of day and may run past midnight.
    Routing Times of day, to the minute, within a single day (00:00 to 24:00). A route cannot span several days.
    Booking Real dates and times, to the minute, over a number of days.

    "Optimal" versus "good"¶

    Job-shop, scheduling, knapsack, packing, booking and cutting can prove that a plan is the best possible. Given enough time they return OPTIMAL. If the time runs out first, they return FEASIBLE together with a gap that says how far off the plan might be.

    Routing works differently. It builds routes and keeps improving them, but it does not try to prove that nothing better exists. So:

    • FEASIBLE is the normal, successful status for a routing plan.
    • OPTIMAL is rare for routing, and there is never a gap.
    • UNKNOWN means the time ran out, or you cancelled, before any plan was found.
    • INFEASIBLE means the engine gave up without finding routes that follow all the rules. This is strong evidence that none exist, but not a strict proof.
    RoutingPlan plan = problem.solver().timeLimit(Duration.ofSeconds(20)).solve();

if (plan.isFeasible()) {
    // Use the plan. Do not wait for isOptimal(): for routing it is almost never true.
}

    Routing¶

    Travel time does not depend on the time of day¶

    The trip from A to B always takes the same time, at 3 in the morning or in rush hour. Travel times come from your own table, or from the distance and one average speed (travelSpeedKmh, 40 by default). There is no rush-hour model.

    Straight-line distances are shorter than roads¶

    By default orkit computes distances "as the crow flies" from the coordinates (the technical name is haversine distance). Real roads are longer. For realistic results, pass your own table of road distances:

    .distances(DistanceSource.fromMatrix(metersByRoad))

    See Distances and travel times for the layout of the table.

    Coordinates¶

    Latitude must be between -90 and 90, longitude between -180 and 180. Anything else is rejected:

    ModelException: Stop 's': latitude must be within [-90, 90], got: 100.0

    A common mistake is to swap the two. Latitude comes first: at(41.39, 2.16).

    Choosing the penalty for skipping a stop¶

    When you allow the solver to skip stops, each skipped stop costs a penalty. The penalty is measured in the same unit as the goal. With the default goal that unit is meters.

    The solver compares two numbers: the extra distance needed to visit the stop, and the penalty for skipping it. It picks the smaller.

    • Penalty smaller than the detour: the stop is skipped, even though visiting was possible.
    • Penalty larger than any possible detour: the stop is skipped only when visiting is truly impossible.
    .allowDroppingStops(50_000)      // skipping costs as much as a 50 km detour

    If a 50 km detour is larger than anything in your area, stops are skipped only when they really cannot be served. If you set the penalty to 500, the solver will skip any stop that is more than 500 meters out of the way.

    With the goal vehiclesUsed(), a stop that can be served is never skipped.

    Scheduling: making preferences matter¶

    A roster can have several goals at once: low cost, fair workloads, happy employees. The solver adds them all into one score and makes that score as small as possible. Each goal has a weight that says how much it counts.

    The trap: cost is counted in large numbers. A shift costs costPerHour × minutes. With an hourly cost of 40, one 8-hour shift counts as 40 × 480 = 19,200. Next to that, the default reward of 5 for a satisfied preference is tiny. It only decides between rosters that cost the same.

    Goal added with prefer(...) Default weight Counted per
    balanceWorkloadAcrossEmployees 10 minute of difference between the busiest and the least busy employee
    honorEmployeePreferences 5 preference that is satisfied (a reward, so it lowers the score)
    penalizeUnderstaffing 100 missing person on a shift
    penalizeContractDeviation 1 minute above or below the contract hours

    Example. A senior employee costs 40 per hour and would like the day shift. A junior costs 25 per hour. The shift needs one person, five days in a row.

    EmployeeRoster roster = Optimize.scheduling()
        .horizon(monday, 5)
        .employee("Senior", e -> e.costPerHour(40).prefersShift("Day"))
        .employee("Junior", e -> e.costPerHour(25))
        .shift("Day", s -> s.daily().from(LocalTime.of(9, 0)).to(LocalTime.of(17, 0)).needs(1))
        .prefer(honorEmployeePreferences(weight))
        .goal(totalLaborCost())
        .solver().solve();
    weight Who works Labor cost Why
    5 (the default) Junior, all five days 1000 Giving the senior a shift costs 7,200 more and earns a reward of only 5.
    10,000 Senior, all five days 1600 Now the reward of 10,000 is larger than the extra cost of 7,200.

    So: to make a preference win against cost, give it a weight in the same range as the cost of a shift.

    Use laborCost() when you want money

    roster.objective() is the combined score, in those large internal units. roster.laborCost() is the plain cost: hours times hourly cost.

    Packing: floating boxes¶

    By default the packing model only makes sure that boxes do not overlap. Nothing stops a box from hanging in mid-air. For a plan that people can actually load, turn on support:

    .requireSupport(true)

    Every box then stands on the floor or sits on top of one other box that is at least as large underneath. This is stricter than real life: a box may not rest across two boxes, and may not stick out over an edge. Some loads that a person could build are therefore not found.

    Cutting¶

    • Piece numbers on rolls are assigned afterwards. When you ask for 8 pieces of Bar-A, the result calls them Bar-A#1 to Bar-A#8, numbered roll by roll. The numbers have no meaning beyond telling the pieces apart.
    • Guillotine layouts are simple on purpose. With guillotine(true), sheets are cut into strips and each strip into pieces. Every such layout can be cut edge to edge, but more complex edge-to-edge layouts are not tried. The result may use a little more material than the best possible guillotine layout. See Guillotine cuts.

    How big a problem can it handle?¶

    Rough numbers from tests on a laptop, with default settings. Each of these was solved and proven best within about 10 seconds:

    Domain Size
    Cutting rolls 300 pieces of 4 types on 90 rolls
    Packing 60 boxes that may be rotated
    Job-shop 8 jobs on 5 machines, with a choice of machines
    Scheduling 12 employees, 21 shifts, one week

    Larger problems still work. Expect FEASIBLE plans with a gap instead of OPTIMAL, and help the solver with a longer time limit or a warm start.

    The details of a problem matter more than its size. Tight deadlines and many rules can make a small problem hard, and a large problem with few rules can be easy. Try your own data.
